The art form

What is
Datamosh?

A guide for the curious — from video compression basics to live performance art.

Datamoshing is what happens when you deliberately corrupt video compression. Not a filter, not an effect preset — a genuine breakdown of how digital video works, weaponised as an aesthetic tool.

The results are unmistakable: colours bleeding across hard cuts, objects smearing through space, ghost images from earlier in the video haunting the present frame. It looks broken because it is broken, in precisely the right way.

"Datamosh is the digital equivalent of double-exposure film. The medium's own failure becomes the medium."

How video compression works

To understand datamoshing, you need to understand one thing about how video is stored: most frames don't exist.

Modern video codecs (H.264, HEVC, VP9) work by only storing complete images at intervals — these are called I-frames (intra-frames, or keyframes). Everything in between is stored as P-frames (predictive frames): instead of a full image, a P-frame says "take the previous frame, move this block here, change that region's colour by this amount." The frame is reconstructed from instructions, not pixels.

I-FRAMES store complete images. P-FRAMES store only the changes from the previous frame.

The glitch

Datamoshing happens when you remove or replace the I-frames. When the decoder hits a cut but finds no keyframe to reset from, it has to keep applying the motion instructions from the old clip onto the new one. The motion data from one scene gets applied to the pixels of an entirely different scene.

The result: colours and motion from the first clip bleed through the imagery of the second. Objects from scene one smear through scene two. The visual information from one moment in time physically inhabits another. This is temporal displacement made visible.

Datamoshing vs traditional glitch

Datamosh

Structural — exploits the temporal architecture of compressed video. The artefacts are caused by genuine temporal displacement. Cannot be replicated with a filter.

VHS / Tape

Electromagnetic — signal degradation from magnetic tape dropout, tracking errors, head switching. Analogue in origin. Horizontal tearing, chroma noise, sync loss.

Pixel Sort

Algorithmic — pixels within a frame are re-sorted by brightness or hue within threshold bands. A single-frame operation with no temporal component.

Feedback

Recursive — the output becomes the input. Zoom, rotation, and translation applied each loop creates infinite regress. Analogue video feedback predates digital.

A brief history

1990s

Analogue precursors

Video artists like Nam June Paik and Steina Vasulka build hardware synthesisers to corrupt video signals. The aesthetic of broken TV becomes intentional art.

2000s

Codec era begins

H.264 and similar codecs become standard. Artists discover that hex-editing video files — removing or duplicating I-frames — produces dramatic temporal artefacts. Early datamoshing is manual and painstaking.

2009

Mainstream visibility

Chairlift's Evident Utensil music video brings datamoshing to a mainstream audience. The codec artefact becomes a recognised aesthetic rather than an accident.

2010s

Live performance

Tools like Avisynth scripts and custom Max/MSP patches bring real-time datamoshing to VJ performances. The aesthetic migrates from post-production to live.

Now

Real-time, MIDI-mapped

huff brings datamosh and related glitch effects to a native desktop app with live MIDI/OSC control and direct output to professional VJ software.

What huff adds

Traditional datamoshing works on rendered files. You export, corrupt, re-import. It's a post-production technique.

huff does it in the frame buffer — in real time, at 30–60 fps, in response to MIDI knobs and OSC sliders. Instead of removing I-frames from a codec stream, huff maintains its own ring of captured frames and samples from them in real time per tile. The visual result matches what codec datamoshing produces, but it runs live and responds instantly to control input.

Then it layers six more effects on top: feedback loops, noise-field warping, scanline displacement, solarise, symmetry folding, and ghost trails. None of these exist in codec datamoshing. huff treats the datamosh aesthetic as a starting point, not a destination.
